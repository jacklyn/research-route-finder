DIY UX research

Research does not always need to be a big, formal study.

There are plenty of questions designers can and should answer directly with users as part of the normal design process. DIY research is meant to make that easier. It gives designers enough guidance to confidently run lightweight research themselves, without turning every question into a full UXR engagement.

The goal isn’t to replace Research. It’s to make sure we’re learning from users often enough, and using our research resources where they add the most value.

When should I do research?

Pretty much anytime you realize you’re making an assumption about the user.

You don’t have to wait until you have a polished design, and you definitely don’t have to wait until the end of the process. Research can help us understand the problem before we design anything, explore different approaches, evaluate concepts and prototypes, or understand how something is working after launch.

The earlier the question, the cheaper it is to answer.

If you’re debating something in a meeting that actual users could answer for us, that’s usually a good sign that some research would help.

Is this something I can DIY?

Probably.

DIY research is a good fit when the question is focused, the risk of getting it wrong is relatively low, and you mainly need directional feedback to make a design decision.

Bring in a UX researcher when the research will influence a major product or portfolio decision, when we don’t understand the problem space yet, when the audience is hard to recruit or highly specialized, or when the method and analysis need more rigor than a lightweight study can provide.

DIY it

  • Usability checks
  • Prototype testing
  • Preference testing
  • Terminology
  • Comprehension
  • Navigation
  • Small workflow improvements
  • Comparing a few design approaches
  • An established pattern in a new context

Partner with UXR

  • Foundational or discovery research
  • New audiences or markets
  • Strategic product direction
  • Sensitive topics
  • Complex behavioral questions
  • Large quantitative studies
  • Justifying a major investment
  • High cost of the wrong conclusion

Not sure? Ask.

A 15–20 minute conversation with Research is much easier than discovering halfway through a study that we answered the wrong question.

One important distinction

How finished the design is doesn’t decide whether research should be DIY. These questions do:

  • What decision are we trying to make?
  • What do we need to learn to make it?
  • How risky is it if we’re wrong?

DIY doesn’t mean less rigorous

It means the scope is small enough that a designer can reasonably plan, run and interpret the research themselves. A few things should always be true:

  • Research questions are written before the study.
  • Participants reasonably represent the intended audience.
  • Tasks and questions don’t lead participants toward the answer.
  • Findings separate observed behavior from participant opinion.
  • Small samples aren’t presented as statistically representative.
  • We’re explicit about what the study doesn’t tell us.

When results are messy or contradictory, don’t force them into a clean recommendation. Sometimes the research tells us we need to learn more.
