Guide

Running a DIY study

A study should exist because there’s a decision we need help making. Here’s how to get from that decision to something the next designer can build on.

PlanSteps 1–4
01

Start with the decision, not the method

Before you open Maze or write interview questions, write down:

  • What decision are we trying to make?
  • What do we need to learn to make it?
  • What would we do differently depending on what we learn?

If you can’t answer those yet, you probably aren’t ready to write the study.

Instead ofWe want to test the new dashboard.
TryWe need to understand whether users can identify which piece of equipment needs their attention and know what to do next.

The first gives you a thing to test. The second tells you what you actually need to learn.

Research questions vs. participant tasks

A good research question is usually broader than what you ask a participant. Research questions guide the study. Participant tasks help answer them.

Research question
Can homeowners successfully change their temperature settings using this new interaction?
Participant task
Imagine you’re leaving the house for the afternoon and want to change the temperature to 76°. Show us what you would do.
02

Check what we already know

Before starting a new study, check whether we already have research that answers the question. Look at previous studies, analytics, customer feedback, support data and research from adjacent products.

We shouldn’t keep asking users questions we already know the answers to.

03

Name your assumptions

What are we currently assuming to be true? Those assumptions become the foundation of your research questions. For example:

  • Users understand what “System Mode” means.
  • Technicians will recognize this icon.
  • Users will know which equipment card to select.
  • Sellers think about their workflow by customer rather than by tool.
04

Pick the simplest method that will answer the question

Don’t make the study more complicated than it needs to be. The tool comes after the question.

Can someone complete a workflow?
Run a usability test.
Why do people behave a certain way?
Talk to them.
Choosing between two UI approaches?
Test the concepts.
What do people actually do in production?
Analytics may be more useful than asking.

Test the thing you actually want to learn

Try not to ask people to predict their future behavior. “Would you use this feature?” and “Would this be useful?” sound reassuring, but they’re usually weaker evidence than watching someone try to do the actual task.

Whenever you can, put participants in a realistic situation and ask them to do something. If you can’t observe behavior, ask about things they’ve actually done before, not what they might do someday.

Not sure which method fits? Take the quiz.

BuildSteps 5–8
05

Recruit for the question, not for convenience

Five perfect participants are more useful than fifty people who don’t resemble the users we’re designing for.

Be explicit about who needs to participate and why: what they need to know, own, use or have experienced to reasonably represent the people we’re designing for.

For specialized Carrier audiences in particular, “someone who works in HVAC” is often not specific enough. A homeowner, installer, service technician, controls technician, facility manager and aftermarket seller can have completely different knowledge and goals.

Be careful not to over-screen, too. If the question is about something any homeowner can understand, you probably don’t need an eight-question screener. If it’s about commissioning a VRF system, “works in HVAC” probably isn’t enough.

The participant criteria should match the decision.

06

Write neutral tasks and questions

We’re trying to learn whether the design works, not convince participants that it does. Don’t explain the interface, teach terminology or give away the language, location or action you’re testing.

Instead ofUse the Schedule button to change your schedule.
TryYou want your house to be warmer when you wake up tomorrow morning. Show us what you would do.
Instead ofWas this easy?
TryHow easy or difficult was this task?

Watch what people do, and ask things like:

  • What would you do next?
  • What did you expect to happen there?
  • What does this mean to you?
  • Tell me what you’re thinking.

Always give people room to tell you something you didn’t anticipate. And if you have to explain the design for a participant to understand it, that’s research data too.

07

Know your prototype’s limits

A prototype isn’t the product. Participants may struggle because:

  • an interaction isn’t fully built
  • expected content is missing
  • the prototype behaves differently from the real product
  • the screen size is unrealistic
  • scrolling or keyboard behavior is different
  • they reach an artificial dead end

If a limitation will affect the task, tell participants what they need to know without telling them how to solve it. When you analyze, separate prototype problems from experience problems.

08

Pilot everything

Never make your participants your QA team.

Before launching, have at least one person who wasn’t involved in writing the study go through the entire experience. Check:

  • Do the instructions make sense?
  • Does the prototype work?
  • Are there dead ends?
  • Does anything accidentally reveal the answer?
  • Does the study work at the screen size and device participants will actually use?
  • Does Maze capture what you think it’s capturing?

A five-minute pilot can save an entire study.

AnalyzeSteps 9–12
09

Know what your sample can and can’t tell you

There isn’t one magic participant count for every study. For a lightweight qualitative usability check, roughly 5–8 representative participants can often surface the biggest usability problems and give us directional evidence.

That doesn’t mean6 of 8 participants = 75% of our users.
It meansWe found where problems exist and what’s happening.

A small study can tell us what is happening and where problems exist. It usually can’t tell us how common those problems are across all our users.

If you want to say how common something is, compare groups, set benchmarks or report percentages with confidence, that’s a quantitative question. It needs a more deliberate approach to sample size, so talk with UXR.

10

Separate what people do from what they say

Both matter, but they tell us different things. If someone says “That was really easy” but took three wrong turns and failed the task, the behavior is the important evidence.

Be careful with preference, too. People are often good at telling us what they like and much less reliable at predicting what they’ll actually use. If someone prefers Concept A but successfully understands and uses Concept B, don’t automatically treat preference as the winner.

Behavior
What actually happened
Attitude
What the participant said or felt
Interpretation
Why we think it happened

Keeping those separate makes the analysis much more defensible.

11

Look for patterns, not interesting quotes

Not a findingOne participant said, “I don’t like that button.”
A findingSeveral participants failed to notice the button.

A memorable comment isn’t automatically a finding. Look across participants and ask:

  • What happened repeatedly?
  • Where did people behave differently than we expected?
  • What caused failures or confusion?
  • Are different types of users behaving differently?
  • What contradicts our initial assumption?

Contradictory evidence is especially important. Don’t clean it up just to make the readout simpler. Sometimes the finding really is “We don’t know yet,” and that’s useful too.

12

Treat Maze metrics as evidence, not conclusions

Maze gives us task completion, paths, misclicks, heatmaps, ratings and preference percentages. Those numbers still need interpretation.

Measurement
80% task completion
Interpretation
Participants understood the workflow

Before you make that leap, look at how people completed the task. Did they hesitate? Backtrack? Stumble into the right answer by accident? Misunderstand what happened afterward?

The number should help you investigate the experience, not replace the investigation.

ShareStep 13
13

Close the loop

Share what we learned, not just what we changed. Every study should leave behind enough that someone else can understand what happened. You don’t need a 40-slide deck. Capture:

  • The decision we were trying to make
  • What we wanted to learn
  • Who participated
  • What method we used
  • What we observed
  • What we believe it means
  • What we’re changing because of it
  • What’s still unanswered

Save it somewhere the next designer can find it. Otherwise, six months from now, someone will run the exact same research again.
